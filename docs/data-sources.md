## Choices the generator makes

- **Countries:** every sovereign country in Natural Earth's 1:50m set, each as itself, except Antarctica, which belongs to none. That makes 197.
  - Dependencies belong to their sovereign on the map, e.g. Greenland to Denmark and Puerto Rico to the United States. Figures are the sovereign's own, so they leave its dependencies out.
  - Disputed areas follow de facto administration: Somaliland → Somalia, Northern Cyprus → Cyprus, Western Sahara → Morocco, Siachen → India. Kosovo, Palestine and Taiwan are their own countries.
- **Figures:** the latest actual value of each, with its year, so years differ between countries and a few are old (Cuba's GDP is 2020). GDP per capita divides GDP by the population of the same year.
  - Taiwan: GDP about $920 billion for 2025 (IMF WEO via GlobalNote, in line with DGBAS's US$39,477 a head) and population 23.33 million (GeoRank's 2025 estimate).
  - North Korea: the Bank of Korea's nominal GNI of $32.01 billion for 2024, standing in for GDP, as it publishes no nominal GDP.
  - Eritrea: the UN's $2.28 billion for 2023, via Worldometer, as the World Bank's last figure is for 2011.
  - Vatican City: population 825 (Natural Earth, 2019), area 0.44 km² (Vatican City State), and no GDP.
  - Taiwan's and Kosovo's land areas are computed from Natural Earth's borders, so they have no year.
- **Capitals:** Natural Earth's, except where `CAPITAL_OVERRIDES` gives the official or seat-of-government city (e.g. Dodoma, Yamoussoukro), `CAPITAL_NAMES` a current or English name, and `MANUAL_CAPITALS` Nauru's, which is not in its list.
- **Small countries:** a country smaller than a map texel never takes a texel from a neighbour, but an island keeps the one it draws on open sea. So Liechtenstein, Monaco, San Marino and Vatican City hold no texels and are reached through search, while Nauru and Tuvalu can be clicked.
- **Regions** are internal, and only drive the density of city lights. Each country's provinces are clustered with population-weighted k-means, the number growing with population and land area, for about 360 in total. A region's population splits the national total by the population of its cities, plus a small area term.

## Solar System — `tools/data/build_solar.py`

`tools/data/bodies.json` is the list of 488 bodies, kept by hand: each one's id, what it is drawn as, where its data comes from, its map and how real its imagery is. Rebuild with `build_planets.py`, `build_satellites.py`, `build_small_bodies.py` and then `build_solar.py`, all run with `python3 -I`. The fetchers keep what they download in `tools/data/.cache`, and their results are the `jpl_*.json` files beside them.

| Data | Source |
|---|---|
| Planets' orbits and their rates per century | [JPL, Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html), Table 1 |
| Planets' and dwarf planets' sizes and masses | [JPL, Planetary Physical Parameters](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/phys_par.html) |
| Moons' orbits | [JPL, Planetary Satellite Mean Elements](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/elem/), turned to the ecliptic |
| Moons' sizes and masses | [JPL Horizons](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/), by satellite code, where it gives them |
| Asteroids, comets and dwarf planets: orbits, names, orbit classes, diameters, GM, albedos, spectral types | [JPL Small-Body Database](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/sbdb.html) |
| Everything else | `tools/data/solar_overrides.json`, each entry naming its published source |
| The Sun | IAU 2015 nominal radius and GM |

Names are the IAU's. A body with no name goes by its designation (`2012 VP113`, `S/2021 N 1`), a numbered one with its number too (`514041 (2014 MQ18)`).

**Estimates.** What no source gives is estimated, and the body's `estimated` list says so: 
- a size from the absolute magnitude, by D = 1329 / √albedo × 10^(−H/5), at the measured albedo or else an assumed one: 0.04 for comets, 0.07 for centaurs, 0.1 for trans-Neptunian objects and 0.14 for the rest;
- a mass from the size, at an assumed density: 0.5 g/cm³ for comets, 1.0 for trans-Neptunian objects, 1.5 for moons and 2.0 for the rest;
- a small moon of no published size, most of them recently found, at 2 km radius. The overrides give rough sizes for the larger ones, from their discovery papers' magnitudes, also marked as estimates.

**Accuracy.** Planets follow JPL's approximate elements and rates, and stay within 1° of Horizons' longitudes from 1950 to 2050 (`src/astro/__tests__/accuracy.test.ts`). Every other body moves on a fixed ellipse from its epoch at Kepler's rate, with no perturbations, so it drifts as the date leaves its epoch. The three interstellar objects move on hyperbolas from JPL's elements instead, within 0.25 AU of Horizons at the dates tested from 2000 to 2040, and show only within 130 AU of the Sun. A moon's place along its orbit is unreliable far from its epoch, especially for fast inner moons.

Mercury's and the Moon's mosaics are photometrically flat, so their relief is a hillshade of the DEM lit from the north-west, mixed into the brightness; Mars's Viking mosaic is made of sunlit images and gets a little of MOLA's on top; Ceres's has only its own. Colours are pulled back from the mosaics' stretch to each body's real tones, keeping the regional differences (Mercury's bluish low-reflectance material and warmer smooth plains, the Moon's bluish maria). Gaps are filled from around them; where a mosaic is poor near a pole (Mercury north of 78° and south of 72°S, Ceres beyond 80°) brightness and colour fade to the average, and Mars's messy caps become clean ice beyond 81°N and 84°S. Jupiter is warmed a little towards Cassini's and Juno's true-colour views, Ganymede's dark terrain made browner; Europa's and Callisto's greyscale mosaics are coloured along a ramp of their real tones (Europa's white-tan ice and reddish-brown lineae, Callisto's dark brown-grey and bright ejecta), their poorly imaged poles fading to the average. The Galilean moons' mosaics give longitudes positive west, −180° to 180°, but are drawn east to the right as ours are, so they need no turning (Loki lands at 13°N, 51°E as it should). Saturn's moons' greyscale mosaics are coloured along ramps of their tones the same way (Enceladus almost pure white, Iapetus's leading face dark reddish-brown), their smeared poles fading to the average beyond 80°. Their labels disagree on centre longitudes, but read as ISIS lays them out (east to the right, x from the projection's centre longitude), only Titan's (0°–360°) needs turning half a turn; checked by Xanadu (100°W) being Titan's brightest equatorial region, Herschel on Mimas's leading face at 112°W and Odysseus on Tethys near 33°N, 129°W. Titan's surface only shades its pale orange haze a little, as in visible light. Voyager 2 flew past Uranus in 1986 with the system's south pole towards the Sun, so its moons were imaged only south of about their equators (USGS's mosaics on `planetarymaps.usgs.gov` hold none of them; JPL's copies do). The maps run east to the right from −180° as ours do, checked by Ursula on Titania (12°S, 45°E), Hamlet on Oberon (46°S, 44°E), Wunda on Umbriel (8°S, 86°W) and Miranda's Arden and Elsinore coronae. The rest of each moon is made up from its own imaged ground: 48-pixel patches of the well-imaged band from 62°S to 18°S, their broad tone taken out, picked at random with a fixed seed and laid overlapping under raised-cosine windows (laid on polar maps beyond 62°, so the poles don't pinch), on the moon's average tone; the shadowed edge of the coverage is trimmed and feathered in, and the mosaics' broad swings of brightness halved. Then they are coloured along ramps of the moons' tones (Ariel's bright bluish grey, Umbriel's sooty dark with Wunda's bright ring, Titania's and Oberon's faintly warm). In these years the Sun stands high over their northern hemispheres (Uranus's northern solstice is in 2028), so the sunlit faces are mostly the made-up ones. Triton's and Pluto's unseen parts are made up the same way (`unseen()`), but start from the tone along the edge of the coverage rather than the average: Triton's far north from its ground between 35°S and 25°N (its brightness on a ramp from grey to pale peach-pink, with half the mosaic's own colour, Voyager's green cast taken out), Pluto's south beyond about 30°S, in polar night when New Horizons came by in 2015, from its ground between 20°S and 55°N. Pluto's map runs 0°–360° east and is turned half a turn (Sputnik Planitia lands at 175°E, Cthulhu to its west); its greyscale goes on a ramp of its MVIC colours, tholin-dark red-brown through tan-orange to the cream of Sputnik's nitrogen ice, the north polar Lowell Regio a little greyer. Phobos's map is coloured along a ramp of its dark, faintly reddish grey, a cooler grey round Stickney (1°N, 49.7°W, where the map puts it) for the bluer unit Mars Express and MRO saw there. Deimos's map leaves what Viking never saw a flat light grey; those parts are found by their brightness (lighter than any ground) and made up the same way, from its ground within 60° of the equator, on a ramp a little warmer than Phobos's. The three asteroids are made up whole by `asteroid()`: an ellipsoid as long as each light curve's swing suggests (Lick about 1.35:1 from 0.25–0.33 mag; Cruithne nearly 2:1 from 0.67 mag, so pinched at the middle into two lobes; 52762 (1998 MT24), unknown, 1.2:1), made lumpy with value noise on the sphere, then cratered with bowls and raised rims (sizes by a power law), scaled to a mean radius of 1. Its radius in every direction goes to `<name>_shape.png` at 512 × 256, 0 to 4 radii in 16 bits (the high byte in red, the low in green, as a browser reads only 8 bits a channel); `bodyArt.ts` lays it on a cube map when loaded and ray-marches it, each pixel's ray stepping in by part of the gap to the surface, lit by the relief's slope, with a second ray towards the Sun for the lumps' and crater walls' shadows. Their surfaces are regolith tones mottled at two scales with brighter young craters: Lick an olivine-rich A-type's reddish grey-tan, Cruithne a fresh Q-type's light grey (albedo about 0.37), 52762 (1998 MT24) an S-type grey-brown. Arend-Rigaux's nucleus is made the same way: about 1.45:1 (its light curve swings up to 0.4 mag), pitted more than cratered, coal-black and faintly reddish (albedo 0.028, lightened a little so its shape reads in the shade steps), with a few paler patches of exposed ice. Charon's greyscale goes on a ramp of its neutral grey ice, with Mordor Macula's reddish-brown tholins tinted in round the north pole where the ground is darkest (as in the MVIC colour views); south of about 30°S, in polar night in 2015, is made up from its ground between 25°S and 50°N. Vesta is drawn by the same ray-casting as the lumpy asteroids, but from its real shape: DLR's shape model gives its radius (212 to 293 km) in every direction, so its flattened south (the Rheasilvia basin and its central peak) and its uneven outline are its own. Its mosaic was taken under a low Sun, so its broad swings of brightness are halved (the shape shades it now), and its far north, dark in winter when Dawn mapped it, is made up from its ground between 50°S and 40°N. The small bodies with published shape models are converted by `mesh_radius()`: each model is a triangle mesh in km in the body's own frame (x towards longitude 0°, z the north pole); points are scattered over every triangle (or, for Phoebe's 3-million-triangle model, its vertices alone), each relief cell keeps the farthest point in it and empty cells are filled from around them, so overhangs and caves (Hyperion's) give way to the outer surface; the result is scaled to a mean radius of 1. Their surfaces come from `shaped()`: a ramp of each body's tones (Eros an S-type's ochre-tan, Phoebe coal-dark with bright ice on its ridges, Hyperion reddish-tan with dark-floored pits, the Trojans Calypso, Telesto and Helene near-white ice, the ring moons a dusty cream), mottled with value noise on the sphere and darkened or brightened in the hollows found in the shape itself. The nine without a usable model are made by `asteroid()` at their measured semi-axes (Mathilde 66 × 48 × 44 km, Halley about 15 × 8 × 7.5, Amalthea 250 × 146 × 128, Thebe 116 × 98 × 84, Proteus 420 × 416 × 402, Larissa 216 × 204 × 168, Puck about 162 across, Nix 49.8 × 33.2 × 31.1, Hydra 50.9 × 36.1 × 30.9), scaled to their mean radius, their lumps and craters made up. Eight asteroids with no shape model or map are made up the same way, by `asteroid()` from the `MADE_UP_ASTEROIDS` table, at their sizes in JPL's Small-Body Database and on ramps of their tones (S-types tan, the four dark ones of albedo 0.03–0.06 coal-grey): Pallas a B-type's bluish grey, 568 × 532 × 448 km, battered by many large but shallow craters (Marsset et al. 2020's "golf ball"; `asteroid()` now takes a crater depth, as a big body's craters are far shallower for its size than a rubble pile's) and flattened in the south by a made-up basin as Marsset et al. suggest; Sisyphus nearly round, as its fast turn and flat light curve suggest. 514041 is unnamed (2014 MQ18). Eris (almost white methane frost, albedo 0.96), Haumea (faintly bluish crystalline water ice with Lacerda et al.'s darker, redder spot on the long axis at 0°) and Makemake (bright ice tinged reddish-tan, patchy) are value noise sampled on the sphere, so they neither pinch at the poles nor seam. Then each goes onto a palette with ordered dithering, as for the Earth: hand-picked, except for Jupiter, Io and Ganymede, whose many hues get a palette fitted to the map by k-means.

Saturn's rings are drawn in `bodyArt.ts` at their real radii (C ring, B ring, Cassini Division, A ring with the Encke Gap) in the plane square to Saturn's pole, with Saturn's shadow on them and theirs on Saturn; Uranus's narrow, nearly black rings the same way, faintly (rings 6–4, α–β and η–δ as three soft bands, then the ε ring, widened a little to show as pixels). Neptune's rings the same way, fainter still (Galle, Le Verrier, Lassell out to Arago, and Adams without its arcs), and Haumea's single ring, 70 km wide at 2,287 km and half opaque (Ortiz et al. 2017). Haumea is drawn as the ellipsoid it is, 1,161 × 852 × 513 km, ray-cast in its own frame and lit by its true normals; Phobos (13.03 × 11.40 × 9.14 km, Willner et al. 2014) and Deimos (7.5 × 6.1 × 5.2 km) the same way, their long axes towards Mars. Neptune's map is bluer than Uranus's, with faint bands, a dark collar near 60°–70°S, a dark spot at 22°N (as Hubble saw in 2018) with bright methane clouds on its poleward edge, white streaks at 20°–30°S and 25°–45°N, and the small Scooter at 42°S. Uranus's map is a pale cyan globe with faint bands, a whitish north polar cap edged by a darker lane near 60°N, a fainter collar round the south pole as Voyager saw it, and a few small bright clouds at 25°–50°N. Venus's map is a cream-white globe with a soft, mottled peach haze across the low latitudes, white wisps sweeping poleward in chevrons, a faint greyer collar and bright polar hoods, made from fixed-seed value noise.

The bodies turn to the IAU's rotational elements (Archinal et al. 2018; `src/app/solar/bodyFrames.ts`), so the maps' longitudes face the right way: Mercury, Mars (the 2009 elements, periodic terms left out) and Ceres (Dawn's). The Moon and the great moons of Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus keep their near sides towards their planets as they go round. Venus turns with its cloud tops, westward in about four days, not its surface; Jupiter and Saturn at System III, their magnetic fields' rates (their clouds have no fixed longitudes); Uranus at Voyager's radio period, backwards, as its IAU north pole is the one on the Solar System's north side; Neptune at its interior's rate, its pole nodding a little. Triton faces Neptune, its pole swinging by up to 32° with its precessing orbit (the IAU's periodic terms, kept). Pluto turns to the IAU's elements (longitude 0° facing Charon). Eris is locked to Dysnomia, its pole Dysnomia's orbit pole (RA 36.17°, Dec 44.51°; Holler et al. 2021) and its day 15.79 days; Haumea's pole is its ring's and Hi'iaka's orbit's (RA 285.1°, Dec −10.6°) and its day 3.92 hours; Makemake's day is 22.83 hours (Hromakina et al. 2019), and its unknown pole is put square to our line of sight (its moon's orbit is seen edge-on) as near the ecliptic's pole as that allows (RA 325.9°, Dec 56.2°). Their prime meridians are unknown and set at 0° at J2000. Phobos and Deimos face Mars, on their mean poles with the largest periodic term of each (1.8° and 3.1°). Eros, Lutetia, Tempel 1 and Phoebe turn on the IAU's elements; Saturn's small inner moons, Amalthea, Thebe, Proteus, Larissa and Puck face their planets on their mean poles, long axes towards them; Mathilde turns in 17.4 days on an approximate pole; Hyperion, Nix and Hydra tumble chaotically in truth and Halley's turn is complex, so they turn steadily at about their rates on made-up poles. Pallas turns on its measured pole (ecliptic 42°, −15°, nearly on its side) every 7.81 hours; Ingeborg, Sisyphus, Öpik and Eric at their measured periods (26.39, 2.4, 6.44 and 12.06 hours); Zeus, Napier and 514041 at made-up ones (none is known); all but Pallas on made-up poles. The three asteroids turn at their measured rates (Lick 5.30 hours, Cruithne 27.31 hours after Larsen 2014, 52762 (1998 MT24) 12.08 hours), on made-up poles. Charon faces Pluto on its mean pole; Vesta turns on Dawn's pole and spin (the IAU's 2015 elements), though the DLR maps' longitudes may sit on an older prime meridian, which would only shift which face is turned our way at a moment. So does Arend-Rigaux's nucleus, backwards in 13.45 hours (Eisner et al. 2017).
